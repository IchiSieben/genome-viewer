Three billion letters, two meters per nucleated cell

A book written in four letters, coiled inside nearly every cell.

3,055,000,000

letters in one copy of the human genomeT2T-CHM13 · Nurk 2022

Reading one letter per second, never sleeping: 97 years for a single copy. Each square is one year.own calculation: 3.055 × 109 s ÷ 31,557,600 s/year

Imagine a recipe book written with only four letters: A, C, G and T. The recipe book of your body is called DNA. It has about three billion letters. Imagine reading one letter every second, never sleeping, never stopping. One single copy would take you almost one hundred years. And your body holds two versions: one from your mum and one from your dad.

T2T-CHM13 assembles 3.055 × 109 bp of haploid sequence, with gapless assemblies for all chromosomes except Y, and the previous reference had left 8% of the genome unfinished (Nurk 2022). Build 35 (2004), which followed the first draft (Lander 2001), reached 2.85 × 109 nucleotides with 341 gaps and about 99% of the euchromatin (IHGSC 2004). A G1 diploid nucleus therefore holds roughly 6.11 × 109 bp (2 × 3.055 × 109). This is our own calculation; it ignores XX/XY differences, the content of Y and individual structural variation.

of DNA in every nucleated cell, stretched outown calculation: 6.11 × 109 bp × 0.3347 nm, rise measured on 1BNA

All of that book is coiled up inside almost every cell. Cells are the tiny living bricks of your body. They are too small to see. Stretch out the DNA thread of one cell and it would be about two meters long. That is nearly as tall as a door. A grown-up has around thirty million million cells. Nobody counted them one by one. It is a careful guess.

DNA length per diploid nucleus is computed, not measured: 6.11 × 109 bp × 0.3347 nm/bp ≈ 2.04 m, our own calculation assuming a 2n nucleus. We measured the axial rise (3.35 Å per base pair) on the coordinates of the structure on screen, 1BNA: base-pair centers, principal-component axis, terminal pairs excluded. Drew 1981 supports the B-helix geometry (9.8 bp per turn). Sender 2016 estimate 3.0 × 1013 human cells in the 70 kg reference man; Bianconi 2013 estimate 3.72 × 1013 and note that earlier values spanned 1012 to 1016. Multiplying gives 6.1–7.6 × 1013 m, a very loose upper bound rather than an estimate: many cells, such as mature erythrocytes, have no nucleus (the hematopoietic lineage is about 90% of cells; Sender 2016). Every nucleus is also assumed 2n, ignoring gametes (n) and S/G2 cells (up to 4n).

Each dot is one letter in a thousand: 12 lit.

Out of every hundred letters, only about 1.2 tell your body how to build its parts. Many of the other letters seem to work like light switches. They say when and where to use each recipe. Nobody knows yet how many switches there are. The whole book is called the genome.

The protein-coding fraction is 1.25% of the genome (declared range 1.08–1.26% depending on isoform handling; computed here from GENCODE 50 CDS on the GRCh38 assembly, not taken from the literature; Frankish 2023 documents GENCODE, not this figure). Yet 88% of trait-associated SNPs in the 2009 GWAS catalog were intronic (45%) or intergenic (43%), although that share is not above expectation in introns and is depleted in intergenic regions (Hindorff 2009); associations concentrate instead in regulatory DNA marked by DNase I hypersensitive sites (Maurano 2012). How much of this non-coding DNA is functional remains contested and depends on the definition of function: ENCODE 2012 assigned biochemical function to ~80% of the genome, whereas Graur 2013 argues that the fraction under purifying selection is below 10%, and Christmas 2023 find ~10.7% (332 Mb) evolutionarily constrained across mammals; Kellis 2014 argue that biochemical, evolutionary and genetic approaches are complementary.

Change one letter in a switch and a light might stay on when it should be off. A computer has learned to guess what such a change does. The Genome Viewer shows you those guesses. They are only guesses, not proofs: someone must check them in a laboratory.

A deep-learning model from Google DeepMind (Avsec 2026) takes 1 Mb of sequence and predicts thousands of functional tracks at up to single-base-pair resolution, although current models often fail to predict the direction of effect of individual variants (Sasse 2023; Huang 2023). Sun 2026, a preprint without peer review, finds weak agreement between Borzoi and the DeepMind model and nominal eQTL effect sizes (Spearman ≤ 0.138), with better discrimination for variants at PIP ≥ 0.9; the authors recommend using such scores to prioritise, not as standalone predictors.
